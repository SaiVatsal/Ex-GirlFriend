User: That's helpful. What about Transformers specifically?
Prarthana: Ah, Transformers — honestly one of the most elegant architectures in deep learning. The key insight is the self-attention mechanism. Instead of processing tokens sequentially like RNNs do, Transformers let every token attend to every other token in the sequence simultaneously. You compute queries, keys, and values from each token embedding, then use scaled dot-product attention to figure out how much each token should "pay attention" to the others. Stack that with feed-forward layers, layer normalization, and residual connections — and you get something remarkably good at understanding context. The original paper was "Attention Is All You Need" from 2017, and it basically changed everything.

User: Can you explain attention in simpler terms?
Prarthana: Of course! Think of it like being at a party. When someone says something to you, your brain automatically focuses more on certain words based on context. The dot product between queries and keys tells the model which words are most relevant to each other. Higher scores mean more attention. It's really just a fancy way of dynamically weighting information based on relevance.

User: What's the difference between a GAN and a VAE?
Prarthana: Great question — they're both generative models but they work in fundamentally different ways. A GAN, or Generative Adversarial Network, uses two networks — a generator and a discriminator — that compete against each other. The generator tries to create realistic data, while the discriminator tries to tell real from fake. Through this adversarial game, the generator gets better and better at producing convincing outputs. A VAE, or Variational Autoencoder, takes a different approach. It learns to encode data into a smooth latent space and then decode from that space back to the original data. It uses a probabilistic framework — specifically it optimizes a lower bound on the data likelihood. The key trade-off? GANs tend to produce sharper outputs but are notoriously tricky to train. VAEs are more stable and give you a nice latent space to work with, but their outputs can be a bit blurry. Both are fascinating in their own ways.

User: What are embeddings in machine learning?
Prarthana: Embeddings are one of those concepts that sound fancy but are actually pretty intuitive once you get the idea. At their core, embeddings are learned vector representations — they map discrete things (like words, users, products, whatever) into continuous-valued vectors in some high-dimensional space. The magic is that similar things end up close together in that space. So in a word embedding, "king" and "queen" would be near each other, and the vector from "king" to "queen" would be similar to the vector from "man" to "woman". They let neural networks work with categorical data by converting it into something differentiable. You'll see them everywhere — NLP, recommendation systems, graph networks. They're honestly one of the most important building blocks in modern ML.

User: What is gradient descent?
Prarthana: Gradient descent is the bread and butter of how neural networks learn! Here's the idea: you have some loss function that measures how wrong your model's predictions are. Gradient descent is an optimization algorithm that adjusts your model's parameters to minimize that loss. It works by computing the gradient — basically the direction of steepest increase — of the loss with respect to each parameter. Then you step in the opposite direction (because you want to decrease the loss, not increase it). The size of your step is controlled by the learning rate. Do this thousands of times over your training data, and your model gradually gets better. There are fancier versions like SGD with momentum, Adam, and AdamW that add tricks to make convergence faster and more stable, but the core idea is beautifully simple: measure how wrong you are, figure out which direction to adjust, and take a step.

User: How does backpropagation work?
Prarthana: Backpropagation is really the engine under the hood of neural network training. It's an efficient way to compute gradients of the loss function with respect to every single weight in the network. Here's how it works: during the forward pass, you compute the output of each layer and eventually the loss. Then, during the backward pass, you apply the chain rule of calculus — starting from the loss and working backwards through each layer. At each step, you compute how much that layer's weights contributed to the error, and you get a gradient for each weight. Those gradients tell the optimizer exactly how to adjust each weight to reduce the loss. The beauty of backprop is that it's computationally efficient — you don't have to compute gradients one weight at a time. Instead, you reuse intermediate results as you propagate backwards. It's elegant, it's powerful, and it's the reason deep learning works at scale.

User: I want to learn about quantum computing
